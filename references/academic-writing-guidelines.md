# Academic Writing Guidelines

Use this reference for engineering-paper Methods, Results, Discussion, Conclusion, limitations, revision, and experiment-related prose across computational, laboratory, prototype, hardware, and field studies.

## Follow the field's language

Use the terminology normal for the engineering discipline. Call a comparison a control, reference, standard method, analytical solution, benchmark, or baseline as appropriate. Describe mechanisms in physical, chemical, structural, electrical, computational, or systems terms appropriate to the study instead of forcing every paper into machine-learning vocabulary.

## Match claims to evidence

Make the central form of rigor claim-evidence alignment. State “improves performance in the tested scenarios” when that is what the experiments establish; do not promote it to “universally superior.” Distinguish descriptive differences from statistically supported ones.

Use cautious wording where uncertainty is real, but do not compensate for weak evidence with dense defensive language. Revise the claim, analysis, or experiment when support is insufficient.

## Write Methods directly

Explain the problem setting, method or governing model, necessary assumptions, materials or specimens, apparatus or numerical setup, initial and boundary conditions, controls or comparators, factor levels, measurement or response definitions, relevant calibration and uncertainty, analysis method, and implementation details required for understanding or repetition. Include only elements applicable to the study. Reuse ordinary project and laboratory records rather than narrating hashes, registries, gates, or evidence chains.

## Write Results around findings

Lead with the most important result. Report the relevant magnitude, trend, comparison, and variability or uncertainty where it affects interpretation; then explain the likely mechanism and what it means for the research question. Mention null or adverse results honestly.

Do not structure Results as a validation checklist, compliance report, gate report, or exhaustive chronology of commands and checks.

## Write Discussion around interpretation

Connect findings to the proposed physical, computational, or systems mechanism, prior work, operating or applicability boundaries, tradeoffs, and engineering implications. Separate observations from model-based or causal explanations. Discuss only limitations that materially affect interpretation or useful future work.

Do not enumerate every imaginable threat or pre-answer hypothetical reviewer objections. When a reviewer has made a specific request, address that request proportionally.

## Keep limitations real and concise

State limitations that change how readers should understand the evidence: restricted scenarios, a narrow population, missing comparison central to the claim, a consequential assumption, or uncertainty that matters. Do not manufacture a catalog of minor engineering imperfections.

## Avoid audit and certification language

Avoid routine use of “rigorously verified,” “strictly validated,” “fully audited,” “evidence chain,” “immutable record,” “provenance-preserving,” “integrity-checked,” “audit-ready,” “contamination-proof,” “fully reproducible,” “defensible,” and “certification.” Use such terms only when they accurately name work the study performed and that work matters to the claim.

Avoid repetitive openings such as “to avoid the possibility that,” “to rule out any concern regarding,” and “to ensure that no potential.” State the actual question and evidence instead.

## Report work without meta-narrative

Use direct research verbs: “implemented,” “tested,” “ran,” “compared,” and “analyzed.” Do not inflate the narrative with “audited,” “froze,” “registered,” or “established an evidence chain” for ordinary work.

## Preserve normal paper form

Write a research paper, not an audit report, rebuttal, reviewer response, or software certification document. Describe the problem, method, evidence, result, interpretation, and relevant limitation in that order unless the target venue requires another structure.
